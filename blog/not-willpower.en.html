Regain is not a willpower problem

A little weight coming back is pharmacology, not failure. This is the easiest moment to act.

2026-09-13

The first thought when a kilo or two comes back is usually the same: "So it was the drug all along. I was never going to manage this." Then the app gets deleted, the scale goes in the cupboard, and six months later there is a new appointment.

This article is about why that thought is wrong. With numbers.

What the drug was doing

GLP-1 drugs act directly on the appetite circuits in the brain: less hunger signalling, earlier fullness, fewer looping food thoughts. What felt like stronger willpower on the drug was those circuits going quiet.

As the drug clears, the circuits return to their old setting. Hunger and food thoughts come back at their old volume. Nothing about your discipline changed. No trial anywhere has found an exception.

But real-world data tell a different story

In trials, about two-thirds of lost weight came back within a year of stopping. In real-world data published by Cleveland Clinic in March 2026, average regain a year later was under one percent of body weight.

The difference: in real life people noticed early and did something. Rebuilt a meal and training routine, or went back to a lower dose with their clinician. Trial participants just stopped, per protocol.

Timing, not willpower, decided the outcome.

"Worth watching" is timing, not a warning

Kept shows worth watching when the 7-day average is 20% of the way back from your lowest weight. Lost 10 kg? That is 2 kg. Caught here, most of it goes back down. Past 40% is where clinicians start talking about intervening again.

So an amber card is not a failure notice. It is "this is the easy moment".
